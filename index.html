Australian food exports · 1988 to 2025

Feeding the world

Australia sends most of what its farms and fisheries produce overseas. Here is what we sell, who buys it, and how quickly that can change.

By Bevan Saldanha · October 2026

$63.0b of food exported in 2024–25, seven times more than in 1988–89
~70% of everything our farms, fisheries and forests produce is sold overseas
181 countries bought Australian food in 2025
$1 in $5 of food export earnings comes from one market: China

01

A $63 billion pantry for the world

Australia grows far more food than 27 million people can eat. Selling the surplus overseas has become one of the country's biggest earners, and it has grown almost every year for three decades.

Where every dollar comes from

Break the 2024–25 total into 100 one-dollar squares and two products dominate. Grains and pulses (wheat, barley, chickpeas, lentils and more) earn $30 of every $100. Beef earns another $26.

Add lamb and other meat and more than two-thirds of our food export income comes from just three groups. Wine, despite its high profile, earns only $4 in every $100.

More than half of every food export dollar comes from grain and beef.

02

What we sell is always shifting

The mix of products changes from year to year. Weather decides how much grain there is to sell, overseas tastes decide which meats are in demand, and trade rules can shut a market overnight.

Beef and wheat trade the top spot, while wine falls and pulses rise

Rank of each commodity group by export value, 2005–06 to 2024–25 (1 = largest). Click a line to follow one commodity, and click empty space to reset

Beef has been number one in 15 of the last 20 years. Wheat takes the top spot after big harvests, as in 2021–22 and 2022–23. Wine was our second-biggest food export in 2006–07 but ranked 10th in 2024–25. Pulses climbed from 13th to 5th. Source: ABARES Agricultural Commodity Statistics, Table 13.

Where the last decade's growth came from

$27 billion of extra sales in ten years

Food exports rose from $35.9 billion to $63.0 billion over the decade. Read the waterfall from top to bottom: each bar adds what one commodity contributed to that growth.

Grains and pulses added $8.5 billion and beef $7.1 billion. Together they explain more than half the increase.

Only one group went backwards: live animal exports, down $0.5 billion. Fewer cattle were shipped live, and live sheep exports collapsed from $245 million to $56 million ahead of the planned 2028 end to the trade by sea.

Who is on the other end of the plate?

Australian food reaches more than 180 countries, but most of it stays close to home. Our biggest customers are our Asian neighbours, plus one very hungry market across the Pacific.

04

A changing table

Twenty years ago Japan was the customer that mattered most. Today it is China, and the last few years have shown how risky it can be to lean on a single buyer.

Japan out, China in

In 2006 Japan bought 22% of our food exports. By 2025 that had fallen to 9%. Japan still buys about $6.5 billion a year, not far off its 2022 record, but other markets have grown much faster.

China's share jumped from 4% to 20%. Southeast Asian markets such as the Philippines and Vietnam also more than doubled their share. The United Kingdom, once a top-five market, now buys less than 2%.

China's share climbed for a decade, then fell sharply

Share of food exports going to the five biggest markets of 2025. China sits on the baseline so its share is easy to compare across years. Click a market in the legend to highlight it (shift-click for more than one)

From 2020 China placed tariffs and import halts on several Australian foods. Its share fell from 26% in 2019 to 16% in 2021, then recovered to 20% by 2025 as the barriers were removed. Meanwhile the United States more than doubled its purchases, from $4.2 billion in 2021 to $9.8 billion in 2025, mostly beef. Source: DFAT trade statistical pivot tables.

Four products caught in the dispute with China

Exports to China before (2019), during (2021) and after (2025) the trade barriers. Hover a dot for the exact value

Wine includes all alcoholic beverages; lobster includes all crustaceans. 2019 barley sales were low partly because of drought. Sources: DFAT trade statistical pivot tables; tariff dates from news reports listed below.

Shut out, then let back in

In 2020 China placed an 80% duty on Australian barley, then large tariffs on wine, and informally halted rock lobster. By 2021 barley sales to China had fallen to zero and wine had dropped from $1.2 billion to $63 million.

The barriers were lifted between 2023 and 2024: barley in August 2023, wine in March 2024 and lobster in December 2024. Barley sales to China reached a 20-year high in 2025. Wine and lobster have recovered only part of what they lost.

When one buyer takes a fifth of everything, a single decision in Beijing can wipe out an industry's main market.

05

What it all means

Food exports are big and growing

At $63 billion a year, food is one of Australia's most important exports, led by grain and beef.

The weather still decides a lot

Good harvests can add billions to grain sales, while droughts take them away, so totals swing from year to year.

Spreading the risk matters

China's barriers showed the cost of relying on one buyer. Growth in the US, Southeast Asia and the Middle East now spreads that risk.
